Lodestar: bankable demand forecasts for mini-grids

What P90 energy-yield reports did for solar project finance, Lodestar does for mini-grid demand.
Team Lodestar · Tushar Agarwal · Brief 03 "Predict mini-grid demand before building" · Track 2, Digital Solutions & AI · Setting B (non-residential: developers, lenders, RBF programmes) · Code: github.com/TusharTechs/lodestar · Live app: lodestar-utfz.onrender.com
Community & target userUser: mini-grid developers planning sites in unelectrified villages (first: Nigeria, Kenya; validated in DRC). Payers: the lenders, DFIs and results-based-financing (RBF) programmes that fund them. Who benefits: households and businesses in the 217,000 mini-grids needed to serve 490 million people by 2030 (ESMAP, $127B).
The problemDevelopers size systems on demand guesses. Survey-based estimates miss individual customers by 65% (CrossBoundary Innovation Lab). Oversizing a 30 kW site by 100% drops IRR from 12.5% to 6.1%; undersizing causes evening brownouts. Root cause: no forecast is (a) built from data that exists before construction, (b) honest about its range, (c) shows how demand grows, and (d) is ever checked against meters. Lenders cannot price what they cannot bound.
Proposed solutionClick any village. Lodestar estimates potential customers from building footprints and OpenStreetMap, then forecasts connections × consumption per connection month by month (growth shapes fitted on metered mini-grids, levels from AMDA 2024 benchmarks), with P90/P50/P10 from 20,000 Monte Carlo draws. It explains the drivers in plain language, produces a load profile, system size and 8760-hour HOMER file, and runs a lender stress test across every draw: grant needed, debt that is safe on P90 demand, breach risk if debt is sized on P50. A deterministic one-click lender memo; no language model generates any number.
Proof of concept (built this weekend)Working web app and API with 20+ villages in Nigeria, Kenya and DRC, live forecasts for any clicked location, a Demand Bureau upload that narrows the band with an operator's metered months, and a public forecast ledger (each forecast hashed and timestamped before outcomes are known).

Honest limits: two sites is method validation; a census-based flat estimate scored 36% on the same sites but gives no range; learning from 12 metered months loses to persistence when shocks hit (Mutwanga conflict). All reported in the app.
Estimated cost & who paysRunning cost: open data only, no API keys; runs on a laptop or a free cloud tier (pre-computed features plus a small model). Revenue (proposed): single-site forecasts free (adoption); a paid Bankable Demand Report per site for developers and lenders (proposed ~$300, under 0.1% of a ~$585k mini-grid); portfolio-screening subscriptions for DFIs and RBF programmes. Lowest-income users pay nothing: they gain from right-sized systems (lower tariffs, fewer brownouts); each report checks the household bill against the AMDA ARPU benchmark.
6–18 month deployment path
  • M0–3: backtest on 2–3 operators' historical meters under the Demand Bureau protocol (site-month aggregates only).
  • M3–6: pre-register forecasts for 10 sites before commissioning in the public ledger.
  • M6–12: score them against meters; integrate with one RBF programme's site-validation workflow.
  • M12–18: 5 countries; paid reports to lenders. DTECH 2027 (March) as the MVP milestone.
How it scalesFootprints and OSM cover all of Africa (and South/Southeast Asia, Latin America). A new country needs only its regional benchmark; Demand Bureau data then calibrates it. Running cost falls per site as forecasts are pre-computed. Open-source and self-hostable: no vendor lock-in, data sovereignty for governments.
Biggest risk & how we test itThin metered data limits calibration and point accuracy. Test early: the M0–3 operator backtest; the Demand Bureau (give-to-get, k-anonymous: ≥3 sites, ≥2 operators per published cell; federated parameter-sharing option; neutral steward such as AMDA) grows the evidence base, and the forecast ledger makes every miss visible.
